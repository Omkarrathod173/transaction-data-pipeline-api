## Part A: Objective Questions (1 Mark Each — 10 Marks Total)
*Answer each question in a single word, term, or short phrase.*

**Question 1**
*Question:* Name the specific histone protein that acts as a linker histone, binding to the outside of the nucleosome core to pull adjacent beads together into a 30 nm chromatin fiber.
*Answer:* Histone H1
*Explanation:* Histone H1 binds the linker DNA and the nucleosome core particle to lock wrapped DNA in place and promote folding into the 30 nm fiber.

**Question 2**
*Question:* Which architectural non-histone protein acts as a domain boundary marker by binding to DNA to block loop extrusion and insulate chromatin domains?
*Answer:* CTCF (CCCTC-Binding Factor)
*Explanation:* CTCF acts as an architectural boundary protein that stops loop extrusion by cohesin, defining topologically associating domains (TADs).

**Question 3**
*Question:* What chemical modification performed by Histone Acetyltransferases (HATs) neutralizes the positive charge on histone lysine tails, promoting chromatin relaxation?
*Answer:* Acetylation
*Explanation:* Adding an acetyl group removes the positive charge from lysine residues, reducing the electrostatic attraction between histones and negatively charged DNA.

**Question 4**
*Question:* What class of chromatin region remains permanently condensed, transcriptionally inactive, and localized at the nuclear periphery across all cell types?
*Answer:* Constitutive Heterochromatin
*Explanation:* Unlike facultative heterochromatin, constitutive heterochromatin remains permanently silenced and condensed in all cell types (e.g., centromeres and telomeres).

**Question 5**
*Question:* Which structural motif present in nucleoporins forms a hydrophobic barrier/hydrogel network inside the Nuclear Pore Complex (NPC) to regulate macromolecular entry?
*Answer:* FG (Phenylalanine-Glycine) repeats
*Explanation:* Phenylalanine-Glycine (FG) repeats in intrinsically disordered nucleoporins form a hydrogel-like meshwork that limits passive diffusion of macromolecules over ~40 kDa.

**Question 6**
*Question:* What signal sequence must a nuclear protein possess to be recognized by importin proteins for translocation into the nucleus?
*Answer:* Nuclear Localization Signal (NLS)
*Explanation:* NLS sequences (rich in basic amino acids like lysine and arginine) direct cytosolic proteins through the NPC via importin receptors.

**Question 7**
*Question:* In an $\alpha$-helix, a hydrogen bond forms between the carbonyl oxygen ($C=O$) of one peptide bond and the backbone amino group ($N-H$) of an amino acid located how many residues ahead?
*Answer:* 4 residues ahead ($i + 4$)
*Explanation:* The regular $\alpha$-helix involves backbone H-bonding between amino acid $i$ and amino acid $i+4$, giving rise to $3.6$ residues per complete turn.

**Question 8**
*Question:* Name the only standard amino acid that lacks a chiral $\alpha$-carbon center and is commonly found in sharp turns/loops of protein structures.
*Answer:* Glycine
*Explanation:* Glycine's side chain is a single hydrogen atom, making it achiral and providing exceptional conformational flexibility required for tight turns.

**Question 9**
*Question:* Which enzyme family actively catalyzes the formation and rearrangement of covalent disulfide bonds during protein folding in the endoplasmic reticulum?
*Answer:* Protein Disulfide Isomerase (PDI)
*Explanation:* PDI assists in proper protein folding by catalyzing the exchange and formation of intra- and inter-molecular disulfide bonds.

**Question 10**
*Question:* What structural component of the bacterial cell wall is targeted by penicillin and missing in *Mycoplasma* species?
*Answer:* Peptidoglycan
*Explanation:* Bacterial cell walls are composed of peptidoglycan; *Mycoplasma* lacks a cell wall completely, making it resistant to cell wall-inhibiting antibiotics.

## Part B: Subjective Questions (2 Marks Each — 10 Marks Total)
*Provide short, crisp, and direct answers (2–4 sentences) for each question.*

**Question 11**
*Question:* Explain the fundamental difference between how Histone Acetyltransferases (HATs) and Chromatin Remodeling Complexes (e.g., SWI/SNF) alter chromatin structure.
*Answer:*
HATs are covalent modifiers that chemically attach acetyl groups to lysine residues on histone tails, neutralizing their positive charge to weaken DNA-histone interactions. In contrast, Chromatin Remodeling Complexes (like SWI/SNF) are ATP-dependent molecular motors that physically slide, evict, or restructure nucleosomes along DNA without modifying histone chemical groups.

**Question 12**
*Question:* Describe the step-by-step assembly order of the histone octamer core guided by histone chaperones.
*Answer:*
Nucleosome assembly begins with the deposition of a preformed $H3-H4$ tetramer onto the DNA thread by dedicated histone chaperones. Following $H3-H4$ tetramer binding, two $H2A-H2B$ dimers are recruited and added to complete the full 8-subunit histone octamer core around which $\approx 147\text{ bp}$ of DNA wraps.

**Question 13**
*Question:* Distinguish between non-polar covalent bonds and polar covalent bonds in biomolecules based on electronegativity.
*Answer:*
Non-polar covalent bonds form between atoms with near-equal electronegativities (e.g., $C-C$ or $C-H$), resulting in symmetrical, equal sharing of valence electrons without partial charges. Polar covalent bonds form between atoms with significant electronegativity differences (e.g., $O-H$ or $N-H$), causing unequal electron sharing and creating permanent fractional charges ($\delta^+$ and $\delta^-$).

**Question 14**
*Question:* Contrast a protein motif with a protein domain.
*Answer:*
A protein motif (or fold) is a specific geometric combination of secondary structures (like a helix-turn-helix) that performs a localized sub-function but cannot fold independently. A protein domain is a larger, contiguous polypeptide section (usually 100–150 amino acids) that can independently fold into a stable, compact tertiary structure separate from the rest of the protein.

**Question 15**
*Question:* Briefly explain the thermodynamic driving force behind hydrophobic interactions during protein folding in an aqueous environment.
*Answer:*
Hydrophobic interactions are driven by an increase in system entropy ($\Delta S > 0$). When non-polar side chains aggregate together in the protein core, ordered water molecules surrounding them are released into the bulk solution, resulting in a thermodynamically favorable state of lower free energy.
